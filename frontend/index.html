        Why does it move?

        The numbers
        In plain English

        Earth circles the Sun once a year, so we look at the sky from a point that keeps changing. A nearby object is seen against a slightly different part of the faraway background each season: that shift is parallax, a yearly wobble. On top of it, the object may drift in a straight line (proper motion). Wobble plus drift is the zigzag you see in the time cube.

        Hold a finger at arm's length and blink one eye, then the other: the finger jumps against the wall. Earth does the same thing from one side of its orbit to the other, six months apart. The nearer the object, the bigger the jump: parallax in arcseconds is 1 divided by the distance in parsecs, so a star 1.83 parsecs away swings by about half an arcsecond, and something 500 AU away swings by about 413 arcseconds (roughly 67 SPHEREx pixels). Because the jump shrinks with distance, a very distant object barely wobbles at all. Its own orbital drift shrinks too, but more slowly than you might expect (as 1 over distance to the power 1.5, in the simulator's model), so a planet a few hundred AU away is dominated by parallax and the tilt must follow that wobble, not a straight line.

        SPHEREx Time Cube

        Stack time

        Real NASA telescope images of one patch of sky, stacked through time, so things that move stand out. Watch the star in the amber ring.

        Far-away stars hold still. Something close to the Sun drifts a little every year. That drift is how a cold, dark neighbour of the Sun could give itself away.

        Check it

        The bright dot climbing the picture is Barnard's Star, six light-years away. The amber line is where star catalogues say it should be each year. The star stays on the line.

        That is the test. When the tool finds a drift that no catalogue explains, it calls it a candidate. A candidate is not a discovery until other wavelengths and catalogues confirm it.

        Your turn

        Open the same images in the full tool, as a 3D cube you can turn. Barnard's Star's motion is already known, so you can check the tool gets it right before you trust it on anything new.
